Reinforcement learning: Q-learning and PPO

1. The one-sentence version

Reinforcement learning: Q-learning and PPO converts a particular structural assumption into a practical prediction or representation.

2. The intuition

Think of reinforcement learning: q-learning and ppo as a compact rule for deciding which observations should influence one another; the rule helps only when that notion of relatedness matches the problem.

A five-item example

If reward=1, next best Q=.6, discount=.9, and current Q=.2, target=1+.9×.6=1.54; with learning rate .5 the update moves Q halfway to .87.

3. The maths in plain English

\[Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)]\]

Q is expected return for action a in state s, α learning rate, r reward, γ future discount, and the bracket is temporal-difference error.

4. What the model is actually doing during training

  1. Explore or choose the largest current Q action.
  2. Observe reward and next state.
  3. Bootstrap toward reward plus discounted next-state value.
  4. For PPO, collect trajectories and clip neural-policy probability-ratio updates.

6. Assumptions and failure modes

Without exploration, early arbitrary choices prevent discovery; reward misspecification can teach behaviour you did not intend. A MIP solver often beats RL on operations problems with known constraints.

8. Where it's actually used

  • Robot or game control in simulators.
  • Adaptive resource policies with careful safety constraints.
  • Sequential recommendation research.
  • Control baselines before model-predictive control.

9. How it relates to its neighbours

Dynamic programming uses a known transition model. Contextual bandits omit long-term state effects. PPO is policy-gradient; DQN approximates Q values for discrete actions.
