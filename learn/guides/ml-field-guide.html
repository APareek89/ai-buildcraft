ML Field Guide

For interviews and meetings where a problem lands and you have ten seconds. Frame first, model last. Every section ends with words you can say out loud.

Approach finder

Tap one answer per question. The result is the shape, the default method, the baseline, and a 20-second answer.

1. What does the business need as output?
2. Is the number about the future over time?
3. Does a wrong answer cost a lot, so you need a range or confidence?
4. Do you have the true answer recorded for past rows?
Answer the questions above.

The loop every problem follows

Courses teach step 4. Interviews test whether you do steps 1, 5 and 6.

  1. Frame the decisionWho acts, how often, cost of being wrong. "Predict churn" is not a decision; "pick 500 accounts a week for a call" is.
  2. Find the target and its biasIs the answer recorded? For all rows? Only sold cars have prices; only called leads have outcomes.
  3. Engineer featuresLags, ratios, cycles, transforms. The target usually hides in a ratio (days since last order ÷ usual gap).
  4. Pick the model by shapeTables → gradient boosting. Text → LLM. Constraints → solver. Need priors or ranges → Bayesian.
  5. Validate like productionSplit by time. Beat the dumb baseline. Report error by segment, not one number.
  6. Turn prediction into actionThreshold, price sweep, budget allocation, guardrails, human override. Then monitor drift and business KPI.
Say: "Before the model, I'd want three things clear: the decision it feeds, the baseline it has to beat, and how I'll validate it on time-ordered data."

The 12 problem shapes

Almost any problem is one of these or a chain of two to four. Learn the shape, and the model picks itself.

Regression a number per item
Examples
Used-car price, order quantity, service time, refurb cost.
Default method
LightGBM / CatBoost. Log-transform a skewed target. Linear or ridge regression if you must show coefficients.
Baseline
Median of similar items (same model, year, mileage band).
Metrics
MAE, MAPE or WAPE, share within ±5% / ±10%, by segment.
Pitfalls
Leakage from post-outcome fields. Errors on rare, expensive items hidden by a good average.
Say: "Gradient boosting on log price, time-based split, baseline is the median of comparables, and I report error by price band because the tail is where money is."
Classification yes/no or a class
Examples
Will this store order this week, is this claim fraud, will this lead convert.
Default method
Gradient boosting classifier. Calibrate probabilities (Platt or isotonic) if the score drives a threshold.
Baseline
Base rate, or the rule the team uses today.
Metrics
Precision, recall, F1, AUC-PR for imbalance. Pick the threshold from business cost, not 0.5.
Pitfalls
Accuracy on imbalanced data (99% by predicting "no"). Target leakage.
Say: "It's a classifier, but the threshold is a business decision — where the cost of a missed case meets the cost of a false alarm."
Ranking / recommendation top-k from many
Examples
Which SKUs to suggest, which leads to call, which articles to show.
Default method
Two stages: candidate generation (item-based collaborative filtering, embeddings, rules) → gradient-boosted ranker with price, promo, margin.
Baseline
"What they bought last time" or popularity.
Metrics
Precision@k, Recall@k, NDCG offline; acceptance rate and lift in an A/B test online.
Pitfalls
Cold start (new store, new SKU) → content features. Feedback loop: you only see clicks on what you showed.
Say: "Narrow then rank. CF finds 200 candidates cheaply, a ranker orders them with business features. Baseline to beat is last purchase."
Forecasting a number per future period
Examples
Demand per SKU per week, calls per hour, revenue per month.
Default method
Gradient boosting with lag and rolling features for many series. ETS / Prophet / ARIMA for a few clean series. Reconcile across hierarchy (SKU → store → region).
Baseline
Seasonal naive: same week last year, or last value.
Metrics
WAPE and bias. Ship prediction intervals.
Pitfalls
Random split leaks the future. Intermittent demand (mostly zeros) needs Tweedie or a two-stage model. Forecast horizon changes the features you may use.
Say: "Lag features into gradient boosting, rolling time-based validation, seasonal naive as baseline, WAPE plus bias so I catch systematic over-forecasting."
Uncertainty / range a number with a band
Examples
Price range for a seller, forecast interval, ROI range for a channel.
Default method
Quantile regression (LightGBM quantile objective at 10/50/90), conformal prediction, or a Bayesian model when you also want priors.
Baseline
Point estimate ± historical error.
Metrics
Coverage (does the 80% band contain 80% of truth?) and width.
Pitfalls
Bands too narrow on rare items. Reporting one number to a stakeholder who then treats it as certain.
Say: "A single number hides risk. I'd give a range with quantile regression and check coverage — an 80% band should catch 80% of outcomes."
Causal effect / attribution what caused what
Examples
Marketing mix modelling, promo lift, price elasticity, "why did sales drop".
Default method
Regression with controls and priors (Bayesian MMM: adstock + saturation), uplift models, difference-in-differences, or a real experiment (geo holdout, A/B).
Baseline
Before/after comparison, or the analyst's story.
Metrics
Recovery of known lift in a test, credible interval width, out-of-sample fit.
Pitfalls
Correlation ≠ cause: ads always run in festive season. No variation → no signal. Multicollinear channels.
Say: "That's a causal question, not a prediction. A model that fits sales well can still get the cause wrong. I'd want controls, priors from past studies, and ideally a geo experiment to calibrate."
Time-to-event when will it happen
Examples
Days to sell a car, time to churn, machine failure, time to next order.
Default method
Survival models: Cox regression, gradient-boosted survival, or a classifier for "happens within N days". Handle censoring (items still on the lot count as information).
Baseline
Average duration for the segment.
Metrics
Concordance index; calibration of survival curves.
Pitfalls
Dropping unsold/unchurned rows — that's the censoring bias. Treating it as plain regression.
Say: "That's a survival problem — items that haven't sold yet aren't missing, they're censored. Cox or a boosted survival model, or simply P(sells in 30 days) if that's the decision."
Segmentation natural groups, no labels
Examples
Store clusters, customer personas, route territories.
Default method
K-means or GMM on engineered features (scale them), HDBSCAN if shapes are odd. Validate by whether the business can act differently per group.
Baseline
The manual segments the business already uses.
Metrics
Silhouette for shape; but the real test is: does the segment change a decision?
Pitfalls
Clustering raw unscaled columns. Clusters nobody can name. Using clusters as the model instead of as a feature.
Say: "Clustering is a feature and a fallback, not the model. Trees learn their own segments; I add cluster_id for cold start and for explaining the output."
Anomaly detection what looks wrong
Examples
Fraud, sensor faults, bad data loads, unusual orders.
Default method
If labels exist → classification. If not → isolation forest, forecast-residual thresholds (predict normal, flag big misses), autoencoders for high-dimensional data.
Baseline
Fixed thresholds and rules.
Metrics
Precision at the top-N alerts (analyst time is the constraint), time to detect.
Pitfalls
Alert fatigue. "Anomalous" ≠ "bad" — new products look anomalous.
Say: "First question: do we have labelled bad cases? If yes it's a classifier. If not, I'd model normal and flag large residuals, then tune for analyst capacity."
Optimization a plan under constraints — not ML
Examples
Delivery routes, budget allocation, shift scheduling, inventory reorder points.
Default method
Solvers: OR-Tools, linear or mixed-integer programming, heuristics (2-opt, simulated annealing). ML only predicts the inputs (travel time, demand, ROI curves).
Baseline
Current plan, or greedy.
Metrics
Objective (km, cost, profit) and constraint violations; adherence by humans.
Pitfalls
Reaching for ML. Writing your own solver. Optimizing a forecast's point estimate without its uncertainty.
Say: "That's an optimization problem. ML predicts travel time and demand; a solver makes the plan. I wouldn't write the solver — OR-Tools."
Text and documents extract, classify, answer
Examples
Contract clause extraction, support-ticket routing, plain-English questions over reports, sales pitch drafting.
Default method
LLM, in this order: prompt → RAG (retrieval over your documents) → fine-tune only for style/format at scale. Small classifier (TF-IDF or embeddings + logistic) if you have thousands of labels and need speed.
Baseline
Keyword rules, FAQ page, current human process.
Metrics
Golden-set accuracy scored weekly, hallucination rate, thumbs-down rate, latency, cost per query.
Pitfalls
Fine-tuning to teach facts (use RAG). No eval set. Prompt injection. Answers with no citation.
Say: "Prompt first, RAG when it needs our documents, fine-tune last and only for format or cost. Whatever we pick, a golden set of 200 questions is the review gate."
Vision read images
Examples
Shelf audit, vehicle damage assessment, document OCR, quality inspection.
Default method
Pretrained CNN or ViT fine-tuned on your labels (classification, detection with YOLO-style models). A vision LLM for low volume or open-ended questions.
Baseline
Human audit sample.
Metrics
Precision/recall per class, mAP for detection.
Pitfalls
Lighting/camera drift. Labels cost more than models. Training from scratch.
Say: "Fine-tune a pretrained model; the cost is labels, not compute. For low volume I'd try a vision LLM first and only train if accuracy or cost demands it."
Two traps: "predict" when the question is "cause" (a good fit is not a cause), and "ML" when the problem is "optimize" (a solver, not a model).

Concepts you'll be asked about

Each one: what it is in a sentence, and the line to say.

Overfitting, bias and variance

Overfit = memorised training data, fails on new data. High bias = too simple, misses pattern. High variance = too flexible, follows noise. Control with regularisation, fewer/better features, early stopping, more data.

Say: "Gap between training and validation error tells me which side I'm on. I'd rather a slightly worse fit that holds up out of sample."
Train / validation / test, and why time-based

Train to fit, validation to tune, test once at the end. For business data split by time: train on months 1–18, validate on 19–21, test on 22–24. Random split leaks the future and flatters the score.

Say: "Random split on a time-ordered dataset is the most common way to lie to yourself. I split by date, always."
Leakage

A feature that contains information not available at prediction time. Examples: rolling average including the current week; "days on lot" when predicting price; a status field set after the outcome.

Say: "For every feature I ask: would this value exist at 6am on the day we predict? If not, it's leakage."
Cross-validation

Repeat train/validate over several folds and average. For time series use rolling-origin folds (each fold trains on the past, validates on the next window).

Say: "Rolling-origin CV, because a single split can be a lucky quarter."
Feature engineering — where the value lives

Turn raw rows into signals: lags, rolling means, ratios (current ÷ usual), days-since, counts, shares, cyclical encodings for week/month, target encoding for categories. For MMM: adstock (carry-over decay) and saturation (diminishing returns).

Say: "The best feature is usually a ratio to the item's own baseline — it normalises size away so the model learns behaviour."
Why gradient boosting for tables

Trees built in sequence, each fixing the previous one's errors. Handles non-linearity, interactions, missing values, mixed types, without scaling. LightGBM (fast), XGBoost (classic), CatBoost (categoricals). Deep learning rarely wins on tabular data and costs more to run and explain.

Say: "On tabular data gradient boosting is the default until proven otherwise. I'd only move to deep learning for text, images, or sequences."
Linear models still matter

Use when you need coefficients a finance team will accept, when data is small, or as a baseline. Ridge/lasso add regularisation. Log-log form gives elasticities.

Say: "If the deliverable is an ROI per channel, I need a coefficient, so it's regression — Bayesian if I want ranges and priors."

Say: "Three layers of metrics: model, product, business. A better AUC that nobody acts on is worth nothing."
Class imbalance

When positives are rare (fraud, churn). Don't rebalance blindly; use AUC-PR, tune the threshold to business cost, use class weights, and sometimes downsample negatives for speed.

Say: "I'd keep the true base rate for calibration and fix the threshold from cost, not force 50/50 data."
Explainability (SHAP)

SHAP gives each feature's contribution to one prediction. Global view: which features matter overall. Local view: why this store got this suggestion. Needed for reps, pricing managers, and governance.

Say: "Every recommendation ships with its top three SHAP reasons in plain words — that's what earns rep trust."
Drift and monitoring

Data drift: inputs change (PSI > 0.2 is a common alarm). Concept drift: the relationship changes (fuel price shock). Monitor four layers: system health, input drift, prediction drift, business outcome. Retrain on triggers, not only on a calendar.

Say: "I watch predicted-vs-actual bias weekly. Three weeks in one direction is my retrain trigger."
Bayesian in three lines

Prior = what you believe before data. Likelihood = how well parameters explain the data. Posterior = updated belief. Computed by sampling (MCMC / NUTS). You get distributions, not single numbers, and you can encode known facts (ad effects are positive, TV ROI is around 2).

Say: "Bayesian buys me two things: I can put what we already know into the model as priors, and I get a range instead of a false-precision number."
Adstock and saturation (MMM)

Adstock: an ad's effect carries into later weeks with decay (100 today, 60 next week, 36 after). Saturation: returns diminish — an S-curve (Hill). Both transform spend before regression. MMM infers contribution from co-movement; it never sees which sale an ad caused. No variation in spend → no signal.

Say: "MMM is regression on transformed spend — decay for carry-over, a curve for diminishing returns — with priors so the answers stay sane."
Experiments vs models

An A/B test or geo holdout gives causal truth for one question. A model gives estimates for many questions. Use experiments to calibrate models (feed lift-test results as priors) and to prove business impact before scaling.

Say: "Offline metrics get it into a test; the test decides if it ships."
Embeddings, RAG, fine-tuning

Embedding: text as a vector so similar meaning is close. RAG: retrieve relevant chunks, put them in the prompt, answer with citations — for your own documents and changing facts. Fine-tuning: adjust the model's weights for style, format, or a narrow task — not for teaching facts. Agents: LLM calls tools in a loop; needs guardrails and evals.

Say: "RAG for knowledge, fine-tune for behaviour, prompt for everything first."
Evaluating LLM outputs

Golden set (200–500 questions with known good answers), automatic scoring (exact, rubric, LLM-as-judge), human review of a sample, and production signals (thumbs, escalations). Run it weekly and on every prompt or model change.

Say: "No golden set, no deploy. Same rule as a registry approval for a classical model."
Cold start and the long tail

New store, new SKU, rare variant — no history. Use content features (segment, category, size), back off to the group average, and flag low-confidence outputs for a human.

Say: "I build a hierarchy: item → group → segment. Thin data backs off a level and gets a wider range."
Selection bias and feedback loops

You only see prices of cars that sold, clicks on items you showed, outcomes of leads you called. The model learns your past policy. Fixes: keep the unselected rows with their outcome, add small randomised exploration, model days-to-sell alongside price.

Say: "Once the model sets prices, the data it learns from is its own doing. I'd reserve a small random slice so we keep seeing the real demand curve."
Batch vs real-time deployment

Nightly batch: predictions written to a table (predictive order). Weekly batch: forecasts with reconciliation. Real-time API: sub-second, online features, canary rollout (service-time prediction, on-the-spot trade-in offer).

Say: "The deployment pattern falls out of the decision cadence. If a rep needs it at 6am, it's a nightly batch; if it's at the counter, it's an API."

Thinking on your feet

Four moves that work for any question, including ones you've never seen.

Move 1 — Frame before you answer

Say: "Let me make sure I have the shape right. The decision is [X], the target is [Y], and the baseline today is [Z]. So it's a [shape] problem, possibly with a [second shape] on top."

Buys 15 seconds, shows judgement, and usually makes the model obvious.

Move 2 — Baseline first, model second

Say: "I'd start with the dumb baseline — [last purchase / median of comparables / seasonal naive] — because half the value is knowing whether the model beats it."

Move 3 — Honest about depth, precise about the shape

Say: "I haven't built that exact one. The shape is [X], the default method is [Y], and the two things I'd watch are [pitfall] and [validation]. I've done that pattern on [closest thing you have done]."

Never bluff a method. Bluff-proof answers name the shape, the baseline, and the trap.

Move 4 — Close with validation and action

Say: "I'd validate on a time-based holdout, report error by segment, and then the output turns into [a threshold / a price / a plan] with [guardrail] and a human override."

If you don't know

Say: "I don't know that technique well. Can you tell me what problem it solved for you? I can reason about the shape even if I haven't used the tool."

Likely questions, 20-second answers

How would you approach a problem you've never seen?

Find the decision, name the target and the baseline, match it to a problem shape, chain shapes if needed, then design validation around what breaks it. The model is chosen last and mostly by the shape.

When would you not use ML?

When a rule gets 80% of the value, when the problem is optimization (use a solver), when there's no recorded target, or when the data has no variation in the thing you're trying to measure.

Gradient boosting vs deep learning?

Tables → boosting: faster, explainable, robust to missing values. Deep learning earns its place on text, images, and sequences, or when embeddings from unstructured data feed a tabular model.

How do you prevent overfitting?

Time-based validation, early stopping, regularisation, fewer and better features, and I check the train-validation gap. Also leakage review — most "overfitting" in business models is leakage.

How do you validate a forecasting model?

Rolling-origin splits by date, WAPE and bias against seasonal naive, by SKU group and horizon. And prediction intervals with coverage checked.

How do you handle imbalanced classes?

Keep the true base rate, use AUC-PR and calibrated probabilities, set the threshold from business cost, class weights if needed. I don't force balanced data.

How do you know a model is good enough to deploy?

Beats the baseline and the current champion on the time-based holdout, feature list reviewed for leakage, error acceptable in every important segment, model card complete. Then a shadow or A/B period before full rollout.

What is a feature store and why?

One place to define, compute, and serve features so training and production use identical logic. It stops the classic bug where the offline feature and the online feature differ.

How do you measure ROI of a marketing channel with no click data?

Marketing mix modelling: weekly spend and sales by region, adstock and saturation transforms, Bayesian regression with priors, calibrated by a geo holdout. It infers contribution from co-movement; without spend variation there is no signal.

How do you price something (car, product) with a model?

Two models and a sweep: predicted market price with a range, probability of selling within N days at a price, then expected profit = margin × P(sell) − holding cost. Pick the peak, add floor/cap guardrails.

RAG or fine-tune?

RAG for knowledge and anything that changes; fine-tune for style, format, or cost at scale; prompt first before either. Always with a golden-set eval.

How do you explain a model to a non-technical stakeholder?

Three reasons per prediction in plain words (from SHAP), the baseline it beat, and the business metric it moved. Never the AUC.

What is drift and what do you do about it?

Inputs or relationships change after deployment. Monitor input distributions, prediction distributions, and predicted-vs-actual bias; retrain on trigger; keep champion/challenger so a retrain has to earn its place.

What would you do in the first 90 days?

Listen and inventory data and use cases, ship one measurable thing on the closest-to-money use case, put the base in place (registry, evals, monitoring), then governance by risk tier.

Rule to repeat: tables → gradient boosting; text → LLM; constraints → solver; need ranges or priors → Bayesian; need cause → controls, priors, experiments.
