From shaking air to a spectrum

Five small steps. Move the sliders. Each step builds on the one before it.

1One shaking dot

Every sound starts with something shaking. This dot is that thing. Up, down, and back again is one round trip.

The dot is shown 200 times slower. The sound plays at real speed.

How often sets the pitch. How far sets the loudness. They are two separate things. “Hertz” only means round trips per second.

2The dot draws a wave

Let paper roll past the dot. The dot leaves a line. That line is the waveform: position over time.

A computer cannot keep the whole line. It checks the dot many times a second and writes one number each time. Each small dot below is one measurement.

3Three waves make one squiggle

Real sounds are many shakes at once. At every instant the air simply adds them. The microphone only gets the bottom line.

Switch waves 2 and 3 off, then on. Watch the bottom line change.

4The matching test

Now go backwards. You only have the squiggle. Which waves are inside it?

Pick a test speed. Multiply the test wave with the recording, point by point. Then add up all the results.

0.00

If the test speed is inside the recording, the two rise and fall together. Plus times plus is plus. Minus times minus is also plus. So the total climbs. If it is not inside, pluses and minuses cancel, and the total stays near zero.

What you have found so far

Every test speed you try leaves a mark here. Drag the slider, or let the page try them all.

5The spectrum, then the spectrogram

A computer runs the matching test for every speed at once. The result is the spectrum. That is all the Fourier transform does.

Each bump is one of your waves from step 3. Its height is that wave's amplitude. The computer fades the edges of the slice first, which keeps the bumps clean.

Spectrogram

A spectrum has no sense of time. So cut the sound into short slices, take the spectrum of each slice, and stand them side by side.

Left to right is time. Bottom to top is speed of shaking. Stronger colour means stronger. Try the claps with a very short slice, then a very long one. You cannot be sharp in time and sharp in pitch together.

This grid of numbers is what a speech model reads. The mel step, next, only reshapes the up-down axis to match human hearing.

Steps 1 to 3 build a sound. Steps 4 and 5 take it apart again.
